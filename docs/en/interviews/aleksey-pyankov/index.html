Aleksey Pyankov

A Team's Design DNA and the Triangle of Results

Russia • Yekaterinburg • Studio

DF

How do you understand design culture? What is at its core for you?

AP

I'll answer informally. Just let me philosophize about design a bit – that's my disclaimer.

I've never split these two words apart. Internally we used "design culture". But there's no real difference – everyone puts something of their own into it.

At the studio level, when a group of designers is working on one shared thing, we call it design DNA, a design code. It's the sum of principles, shared goals, missions, views on design and everything around it. It unites us as a group of people who work together. It's a kind of glue that holds us together, the thing we stew in.

I try to look wider – I don't separate the designer from the cultural context. It's part of something shared. Everything around us is design in the broad sense. I try to absorb patterns from everything and then wrap them into project work.

Every moment hurts somewhere – and that's what you pick up on. But there's a general look and feel – that's the term we've been using lately. How does it look? How does it feel? At the level of characteristics.

Something fresh, different, with some innovation in it – that resonates more. Over time, a stale visual language gets tiresome and boring. We look for what gives off freshness.

On the other hand, that's how Pragmatica came about – it's balance, something timeless. We look for something that can live outside of trends, something structural and workable.

Patterns matter. Something that drives us, that's interesting. If something clicks – then it's already something that makes sense.

The worst thing, what drops out of focus and doesn't register in design culture, is something mediocre and unnecessary, empty shells with no meaning. It's better not to make those at all.

Design DNA is the glue we stew in.

– Aleksey Pyankov
DF

Why did you choose design as a profession?

AP

I have a presentation where I talked about my path into design to beginner designers. But the thing is: when I started, I wasn't thinking about design as a whole at all. I started drawing. My path went more through illustration – for many digital designers the path goes through illustration, especially back then.

Whatever I liked visually, I wanted to repeat, I reached for references. I redrew skater graffiti stuff. As a schoolkid, I started drawing on T-shirts and selling my prints. From there I jumped to collaborating with T-shirt and clothing brands. My first job was as a designer at a local Yekaterinburg retail company.

That's how I got into design, simply because I drew. And then I started to absorb that design is much wider than just what's drawn. Over time I realized I needed to enrich my education with something more systematic. In my master's I got a classical design education. My first degree, actually, was social work.

You can come to design later – and that confirms how important a general cultural background is here. A good designer, especially a good art director, is someone who has seen a lot, lived through a lot and rethought a lot. That cultural experience lets you design something – it's easier for you to relate yourself to other people's behavior.

A good designer, especially a good art director, is someone who has seen a lot, lived through a lot and rethought a lot.

– Aleksey Pyankov
AP

One of the brightest cases is a super ancient one, from when I was neither a designer nor an illustrator yet.

At school we had gatherings – all the classes from across the city came together for one big event. There was a particular theme, and we had to give a presentation: how you see the future of your school. They give the task – go off to separate rooms.

We messed up somehow: instead of doing anything, we sat and played cards. Deadline, five minutes, time to go out, and we just have a blank sheet. And we came up with nothing better than going out with a blank sheet. It's a funny insight: the presentation itself and the context let us go out and actually present a blank sheet, and it sounded convincing. We pulled something together: "We're like a blank sheet, and so on." It came across as convincing, and nobody even looked at us like fools. There's a photo of us dressed in the dress code, holding just a blank A4 sheet.

That case formed an approach for me: design isn't only the carrier you drew; it's overall how you presented it, how you launched it. Drawing something is only half the job.

DF

This point about presentation and delivery is really cool. It's a separate entity of its own.

AP

The second discovery was the moment at my old job when my boss came to me: "Lyokh, can you do 3D at all?" I said no. He said: "We need to open a store in Ulan-Ude. They asked us for a visualization. Shall we do it?" I sat down to look for tools. I hadn't opened anything at all. I found what I could master in the time I had.

In the end I made my first 3D visualization of a store. Later I made visualizations before renovating my own apartment. That moment, when some skill of yours scales nicely onto a new medium that didn't exist before, was a pretty cool case. After that I made several more visualizations, and they helped us launch. I later saw a store open following my own layout. A really cool insight.

Among recent studio cases – the first make I led. My strongest lessons came when I learned more from the person I was working with than what we gave them. The coolest case is when I learn something. Over time you share more now. But the most valuable thing is when you absorb something.

Design is a process of discovering the world as a whole, a continuous process. That's what drives me.

Drawing something is only half the job. Design is overall how you presented it, how you launched it.

– Aleksey Pyankov

DF

I'd like to talk about the rituals and practices that help you stay afloat.

AP

There are two aspects. There are rituals that help with work in general – they're needed to keep the work going. And there are studio practices needed to keep the process going at all. It's a way to reach optimal work.

A very important thing for all designers is self-time management, planning your work calendar. Where should I start the day so that by the end of it I arrive at something? You need to picture it: what do I need to do to end up with cool design at the end of the day. My practice is that every week starts with me looking at my little calendar and figuring out how to live with it. It helps a lot.

Organization helps me a lot. Often, when diving into the context of a task, I try to move it all onto one board in Figma. Business requirements go there, contextual things, what's going on with the look and feel. I try to spread things across different criteria and aspects. If I'm working with references, I need to get to the point of formulating the principle – why exactly these references resonated here.

I work in creative chaos, but in some form there's still a moment when I try to arrive at systematic order. That's my ritual. We always start with it.

DF

Tell me about principles and how they translate into design decisions. How is design search organized for you? What do you look at first?

AP

There are personal principles and approaches. And there are studio ones, which we shape. Some of the principles are on the website, some are internal and less formal. And it's a changeable thing.

What's super important besides the challenge is the story about impact, about focusing on results. That's a very important approach overall. You need to think about what it will lead to. It's about metrics, but there may or may not be a metric; depending on the task we might not define one. But if there isn't one, we think about what it will lead to, what the benefit will be.

We came to this scheme – a triangle. What do we get out of it as a studio, as designers? What does the client get? What does the user get? And what does the design community as a whole get? If all these elements get something, then we're doing something not thoughtless. We try not to work in favor of just one party. Of course, someone gets a little more – the client, us or the user. But that's just reality.

The approach to visuals is very important to us. In some places we're even ready to sacrifice functionality if it's going to be good. We're all driven by objects that are brilliantly made and look great. So the drive toward expressiveness is also super important.

DF

Teamwork means the opinions of a lot of people. Do you articulate quality in some way? Or is it a fluid substance?

AP

Top-tier, cool design – I wrestle with it almost every day. I haven't come up with anything better than this triangle. Quality here is a kind of balance: you press one side and another falls off. There's no final criterion of quality.

Quality is that which must not be bad. If it's bad, it's not good. At that level. Then – argue why it's bad.

Ideally, the team defines what's good and what's bad. It's shaped for a specific project, depending on the inputs and challenges there. There's a set of inputs – and based on them, the art director's job is basically to define the criteria: what's a yes, what's a no. Through dialogue with the client, through brainstorms, workshops with the team, interviews with potential users, and you define what's good and what's bad.

DF

These days there's the whole thing with neural networks and AI. How do you use AI in your work? If your process involves craft, drawing something by hand – is there a balance, or does something outweigh the other?

AP

(points that need verification) I really love AI. It has cut down routine a lot – some things that used to take days are now done quickly.

At the same time, I don't think design work can be entrusted to AI. For me it's more of a really cool addition. I don't like it when everyone on Twitter posts the lowest-quality AI pictures – that doesn't make the world better, on the contrary, it creates a pile of low-quality content.

I'm not afraid that this thing will replace me. But everything is developing so fast that sometimes it seems like if you take a year's pause in your career, you'll come back to a world where everything works differently. I think that's a toxic thought. I believe human labor will be valued. And where it stops being valued, those aren't places I'd want to have anything to do with.

We use AI and will use it even more. But in studio work, it's always prompts that we write, refine, and finish on top with our own hands. AI doesn't yet deliver the same quality of work as a human. I hope AI will replace me on routine tasks, where you can give it a template. But the creative, meaningful part – concepts, ideas for improvement – will stay with people, not artificial intelligence.

DF

That's a very ethical and responsible approach. It's strange to fear technology just because it speeds us up. What matters is taking responsibility for how you use it and not creating too much noise and emptiness around your work.
