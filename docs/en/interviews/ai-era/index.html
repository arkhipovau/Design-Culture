Designer as Curator

2023–2025 • Today

The last of nine stories is about the time in which I am writing this. AI is rapidly changing the tools we work with, but the longest pauses in conversations with the protagonists happen when the topic turns to the meaning of these changes. Those pauses are what I wanted to capture here.

AI and the identity crisis

With the mass adoption of generative AI (ChatGPT, Anthropic, Midjourney), design culture faces an identity crisis. If a machine can generate interfaces, write copy, and create visuals — what does a designer do? The answer crystallizes in a redefinition of the role: the designer becomes a critic, a curator, a strategist — not a producer of forms.

This means that design culture is shifting from production to reflection. Questions arise: why this solution? who does it serve? what are we losing? what assumptions are we making? Ethics moves to the center, not the periphery.

Ethics moves to the center, not the periphery.

Speed and longevity

Design culture today faces a clear contradiction. On one side is the culture of speed: AI enables rapid generation of variants, companies want fast iterations, constant tests, A/B experiments. On the other side — a return to slowness: the understanding that quickly generated design degrades, that design debt accumulates, that users grow weary of constant changes.

This tension remains unresolved and defines design culture in 2025. How to hold both demands? How to be fast yet thorough? How to experiment without losing consistency? The questions that troubled designers nearly 100 years ago remain relevant in a new context.

Accessibility and sustainability

Accessible Design and Inclusive Design are no longer niche practices. They are becoming baseline requirements, built into the foundation of the design process. This means that care for diverse users — people with disabilities, the elderly, children, people with different cognitive abilities — is at the center of the work.

There is a growing understanding that Sustainable Design is not only about ecology. It is about the ability to create systems that endure, that do not require constant reworking, that resist trends. A direct return to Rams, but in the context of 2025: in the era of AI, fast cycles, and constant updates, sustainability becomes a rarity and a value.

Lines of history converge

The history of design culture is a history of systematically redefining what a designer does, whom they serve, and how that service is articulated and transmitted. Each period brings its own understanding but does not cancel the previous ones. They layer, intertwine, and find new expressions.

From the Bauhaus, which asserted that design is a collective practice and ritual, to Ulm, which showed it could be a methodology. From the American corporate school, encoding values into systems, to Rams, who formalized ethics. From Apple, translating Rams's principles into digital, to Material Design, which brought those principles into the public domain. From Spotify, demonstrating how to scale culture through horizontal structures, to Uncommon, redefining design as cultural intervention.

Today, these lines intersect once more. AI provides tools that change the nature of work but do not change the questions: how to create with integrity? how to transmit values? The designer, facing a machine that can generate forms, returns to the questions the Bauhaus posed: why should this even be created?

The fragmentation of design culture arises not from a lack of information. It arises from the absence of a unified framework for sense-making. These nine stories are an attempt to sketch such a framework. Alongside twenty-one voices of contemporary protagonists, they should remind us: behind every word about culture in a studio or product stands a century-long tradition of reflecting on what a designer does and why.

The designer is being redefined as the one who asks the right questions, not the one who produces the right answers.
