Elena Chinakova

Building Her Own Design Community, Zorky, in London, and the Designer's Role in a Team

United Kingdom • London • Community • Project

DF

How do you define design culture for yourself? What does the concept include – principles, values, rituals within the team, an approach to decision-making, accountability – or something else?

EC

In my view, when we talk about design culture in a company, it all starts with the role of design in it: as a department or an entity. There are places where design gets a lot of attention and weight, where decisions are driven by important principles, checked, tested, conclusions are drawn, and the designer's word is taken into account (whether it's product or communication design).

And there are, on the contrary, companies where design teams are more like "hands." At first the difference isn't always easy to spot. It hides in small things and then spreads to the big decisions, which can no longer be missed.

After the role of design in the company comes everything else: what design is, which of its aspects matter. I think this also flows step by step from art directors down to the designers themselves.

Over my working life I've seen how much the same team can differ under different art directors: the quality of their work, how closely they follow the rules, and even how much they enjoy the process!

Design culture is also about which approaches and principles a team or brand communicates. Sometimes you look at a company and think, "yes, I like their design culture!" For me, one such company is Spotify.

DF

A bit about your path: why design, and what drives you most about it now? It would be interesting to hear 1–3 cases that especially shaped your perception of design or your approach.

EC

For me these are slightly different questions, but they lead to the same place: design is a super broad field and profession, you never get bored in it, you can "dig" deep, or try different things "wide," or do both.

All my life I've been a person of more than one interest: I have a lot of curiosity about life, about the things around us, how they are built and why they work the way they do. I think design answers those questions in many ways: everything we see can't exist without design, even "no design" is also design. Sorry for the tautology :)

I have lots of ideas in my head, constantly. Design helps me bring them to life and discover new approaches to them. As a child someone told me about this profession, though in the context of interior and fashion design. Before entering the School of Design I knew little about other directions, but I knew clearly that I wanted to be a designer. That's what happened, and I don't regret it at all!

I had many "passions" across directions: I liked making books, posters, visual research – and in digital I found a lot to love too. But when I started working in my 4th year at the School of Design, I "landed" in Avito's marketing team, and there I learned more broadly what communications are and what design they need. I came as an intern and over the course of my work went through every step of the career path within the company – I grew into a design lead with my own team of senior designers. We worked on design and creative for special projects – and here I got to know and fell in love with creative, a notion already beyond design but closely tied to it. Special projects included everything I started with above – variety and "you never get bored."

A music video for HRs? No problem – we write a track and go shoot in a field ("Rabochiy Kh_or"). Need to tell users about the beauty segment? Great: we collaborate with a contemporary artist and make an exhibition at MMOMA ("Beauty at Any Moment"). We can even publish our own magazine in a run of 500 copies (Trendfest)!

I think what drives me is the chance to live a professional life that isn't boring, to be an explorer of the world around me and bring ideas to life. Design is a direct helper in that!

And I also love typography. Once this love produced a funny project that the best typographic publications, studios and type designers in the world followed organically :) (Types_Faces)

DF

On personal growth: which external influences (teams, community, mentors, books) have been the most significant for you? How have your sources of inspiration changed?

EC

First the classics: all the designers and authors we learn about from our curators and teachers. I came to the School of Design completely from "zero" – so at first I simply immersed myself in the environment, finding out which top studios and designers exist in Russia and the world. Then the list grew – I'd say through common sense and visual literacy. Now the whole stack of people I "follow" lives in my Instagram, haha.

I won't give names, the list would be too long, but I want to highlight how my attitude toward inspiring professionals, and the way I watch them, has changed!

You can admire the "craft" or the approaches, how authors and designers reinvent methods and convey their discoveries through design. That part of me is still alive; I constantly find new solutions that I make a note of, or I simply enjoy and marvel at the genius of thought and deed!

You can also be "enchanted" by success: that happened to me in my student years, romanticizing professionals and striving for the same level (which in healthy measure is good), but it's important to remember that super talented authors are not always pleasant to work with (as it turned out), and the path they took doesn't always translate to you: you never know what they had to give up to get where they got (maybe they know nothing but work, or have no close friends). In short, I definitely wouldn't measure success and happiness only in recognition, awards and the like. Sometimes a "pretty picture" already does its job by inspiring you. And it's not at all necessary to strive toward it without looking back.

DF

Which work practices or rituals stay constant? What sustains and energizes you the most? Is there a recurring pattern you notice?

EC

Hm, probably what energizes me most is the process itself. I like coming up with ideas, spinning them up, and often – not agonizing over them in my head for long. Straight to action: make something quick and dirty, or very fast, and refine it afterward – that's my approach. An idea can come at any moment: that's why I always have a Telegram channel whose only subscriber is me! I toss ideas in there, then revisit them and implement some.

At the same time, once work on a particular thought is underway, another part kicks in, which I call structure. Creativity and structure live in me equally; I can be a spreadsheet person and sort everything out for those who are confused. So during development I switch to that stream: being gradual, keeping files in order, leaving notes and so on, so that other visitors to, say, Figma can understand what's what. It has become a kind of standard for me. And of course I expect it from the other designers I work with too :)

DF

How did the idea of Zorky come about, and what makes this format especially valuable to you? How do you choose topics for workshops and meetups? What impact has the project had on you personally and on the community of designers in London? What do you consider the community's mission?

EC

For three years I worked fully remotely, never once meeting my team – I got very tired of that format and of the whole "I'm here, my team is there" situation. I really wanted personal contact. At the same time, after moving to London I saw what a great design and creative community had gathered here. It really isn't easy to move to London. And I was lucky that the people around me turned out to be remarkably like me and like what I share: an open view of the world, inspiration in small things, a drive to make something cool!

That's the first key to Zorky.

The second is the essence itself. Why "Zorky" and what's the concept?

I'm used to living and studying within the paradigm of "serious" design: it awaits all of us at work, where there are many constraints and not always much creative freedom. But design is much broader, and I really wanted to give it a place just to be fun, to do cool things without looking over its shoulder at the rules, to break them, to approach it the way a child picks up a brush for the first time.

Zorky combines new knowledge in design and art disciplines, but it also becomes a place you could call a leisure venue. Come, laugh, relax, and make something together. Without sliding into a "paint and wine" format, where leisure becomes the main thing. That's why different specialists, co-authors, come to Zorky. Together with them I devise the workshop program and then they run it!

Community-based. With a keen eye on life and a chance to "touch" design and art from different sides!

The mission – I'd say "to introduce people to design and art disciplines in a relaxed setting, to make fun things, with no competition, to learn to look at the world from a new angle, to connect great people." All aspects matter!

The project made a strong impression on me: here my husband and I are running the first workshop, with the first 6 people attending. And now 150 people have been through our workshops, and the Instagram account has grown organically to almost 1,500 followers. And now co-authors themselves write to us because they want to collaborate! And suddenly our latest meetup takes place with 100 people in one room!

It surprised me that my love of design and this view of the world led me to create and grow a community. It's surprising that despite the difficulties we get so much great feedback. I like that we managed NOT to turn it into a school, and found a balance of leisure.

It's interesting that all this is also happening in London!

All in all, it's definitely an amazing case, even for me!

DF

How would you characterize design culture and the professional environment in London? Are there differences in approaches and values compared with Moscow?

EC

So far it's hard for me to speak about design culture itself, how things stand in companies – simply because I haven't yet worked in any. But I can definitely say that the difference in design code is very visible. I've noticed it since my first day here.

1. The old world. Plenty of preserved examples of design on the city's streets: pubs, signs, plaques on walls, urban typography. Even in vintage shops you can find lots of magazines and posters from the last century! It's very interesting and curious to study it all!

2. The approach to advertising: since I spent a lot of time in a marketing team, I'm closely familiar with advertising design and know a lot about it. And here the difference in the styles and approaches I see is striking. Clean layouts, sometimes the emphasis only on text, or conversely only on a photo (which in Russia you only see from fashion or brands aimed at a progressive young crowd). Here you can come across such advertising from a home hardware store. A lot of good design and layouts!

3. The international environment: neighborhoods where only Italians live, only Jews, pan-Asian districts. This also affects the visual environment: you walk into a cafe in Chinatown in Soho, after a 15-minute queue at the entrance, you get a menu in a laminated folder, with a pile of characters and tiny English captions for the dishes – and suddenly you're not in London but in Tokyo.

I really like London, and especially these visual parts and finds of it. It's alive, modern, yet pays tribute to history. Everyone will find inspiration to their taste.
