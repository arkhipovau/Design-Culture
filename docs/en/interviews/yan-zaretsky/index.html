deFindings

Yan Zaretsky

A brand's genotype and phenotype, and wholeness as a criterion

Russia • Saint Petersburg • Studio • Product

DF

What do you understand by design culture? Principles, values, an approach to decisions, readiness to take responsibility?

YZ

In my head I'd split design into two notions. There's the applied, craft component – I graduated from an art and industrial academy, where they taught us that design is an applied thing, that there has to be a use. That is, design is a tool. And we, as masters, as craftsmen, should treat it as a tool, including all its derivatives. It doesn't matter what you work in: Photoshop or Figma. Essentially it's a tool for business, for solving problems.

That's where I started, and I really love this approach. It helps you feel in demand, do something useful and through that influence something. You made a thing that didn't exist before, and you see how it changed something in people's lives or in their business. Preferably for the better. And that moment of influence is always the most interesting. Because design is never about mockups. A mockup is project documentation and nothing more. I was never especially interested in mocking things up, it's technical. If a shoemaker makes a pair of shoes, what's interesting isn't the pair itself, though it can be beautiful and pleasant. What matters is how they're worn, who puts them on, how they're used.

There's a second part, the cultural influence of design on the environment, on people, on society. I came to it much later, and I like reflecting on it. The modern world is the heir of postmodernism. People within the consumer system began to simulate and design new values. We don't just buy a phone, we buy a particular phone that matters to us in some way. Someone loves Apple, someone loves some other tech thing. We endow utilitarian objects with cultural value.

Then you can debate whether that's good or bad, how it affects the environment and society. The point is that this is reality, and it's made of things. That's probably the whole second half of the twentieth century. Designers in this sense influence the environment quite significantly, because design has turned into a tool of business and marketing. And how we design products affects what value they carry, literally down to people's perception of reality and their character.

I like the very fact of influence. After that, in some places it goes to the minus, in some to the plus. Social networks, manipulation, marketing, dark patterns – that's design too, just when it's used not for good, consciously or not. So design has two components: the craft one (about beauty, execution, technique, polish, mastery, craft) and the cultural one (about the perception of the world). It's especially expressed now in the digital environment. Digital is super interesting in general: everything is in phones, in apps, everything gets mixed together. I try to do the most interesting projects, the ones with more of that influence, where you can do something meaningful.

Design is never about mockups. A mockup is project documentation and nothing more.

– Yan Zaretsky
DF

Why did you end up doing what you do – you came from graphic design and wound up in digital in the broad sense? What drives you most right now?

YZ

When I graduated, I tried to get a job and it didn't go very well. They'd ask me: "So who are you? What do you want, what can you do?" And when you graduate, it's not always clear what you want and can do. Some people decided and found themselves. I hadn't found myself at all back then. Working with typography seemed interesting, and doing some branding. At the same time I always really liked various digital things, but web design was in such an embryonic state then that it was unclear what to even approach. Branding, graphics, posters, or who even are you, what are you.

At one studio I had, let's say, an interview. They shrugged and said: "You haven't decided. Well, when you decide, come back. For now it's unclear why we'd hire you, because it's not very clear what to assign you. You seem to do everything, but nothing at a good enough level." The story turned out to be fairly cyclical. About five years later I tried to get into a studio again. The web design department turned me down: "You have some logos in your portfolio, and we need a web designer." And the same studio's branding art director turned me down: "You have websites in your portfolio, and we need a strict communication guy: branding, advertising posters, all that." It was upsetting. But my point is that I've always liked the broad field of design. Even though my education is in graphics, I really love looking for patterns and working in different environments and genres.

Not in all of them, of course, you can't cover everything. But I really love everything connected with letters and communication, with language, when design is like a language. And I really like modern technology. So everything digital, websites, interfaces, the more complex the better. When it's the beauty of a system and some structure, I climb in, I really enjoy figuring it out. And the best is when it all comes together.

Right now I have a service for geologists: super abstract, very complex, with industry terminology and user habits. Designing an online service like that is a separate challenge, when you have to dig deep and immerse yourself in the business. And I really love that, immersing in each business, because uniform and similar things are not interesting. It's interesting when a business has texture, expressiveness, its own foundation, and on that basis you can make expressive design.

I like product design because it relies on data, research, scenarios, metrics. Essentially it's a diagnosis of the benefit we bring. In my view, these principles apply to any design. It's like saying: I'm designing a chair, I want it to be comfortable to sit in, with support for the lower back and neck, and then it's all measured in metrics. The product approach tries to understand whether we succeeded or not. It makes sense for every designer to think about that, if they care about the effect of their work.

And web is a more expressive thing. There it's already about mastery, it's more visual, it has character. It's a mix of digital technology and branding, when through grids, typography, text you can make something expressive.

Separately, I do branding – letters, icons, magazines. It's all tremendously interesting. I love branding partly because it largely goes offline. Despite all my love for digital, offline things – books, publications, packaging – you can touch. It's a different experience right away: tactile, olfactory, you hold the object in your hands. Digital can't offer that – let it envy traditional print design. For that reason I love it too, but that's more of a hobby.

DF

Which projects were turning points – changing your perception of the profession and your approach?

YZ

We can start with offline. I have a fantastic engineering book – project documentation. At a plant they service gas turbine engines, and for the generator of such an engine the client produced documentation on the occasion of its delivery to production. A gift edition, which was later given to top executives.

At the start, the client came to me asking me to change the font in Word: Times looked somehow unattractive. "Yan, take a look at what can be done there." And I proposed this kind of engineering book. I worked with the content, split it into two book blocks. The left one is the description of the technical process. The right one is illustrations for the entire process. First the unit is taken apart, inspected, then assembled back. And all these processes are illustrated here and described there.

That's exactly what I love to pull from the web: through cross-navigation between two halves. From the left block, references to the right; in the right one, in the column, references to the different places in the process where that drawing is involved. Names of parts, tools. All of it in a graphic-design key, with abstract icons, with different paper. Not print, tinted paper, four kinds. Thread-sewn binding, stitched with red thread. Both blocks are attached to one cover, so the book opens in two directions.

I really love it when design works with the material. The object is content and material. If you can throw something out and leave only the material, I always prefer that solution. I like the texture of paper, I don't like it when everything is sealed. Same in branding, in web: if I can put in only text, I put only text. If I can leave it unwritten, I'd rather not write it. Because of that, everything drifts toward minimalism.

The web-style navigation in the book also solved a technical problem. There were about a thousand images. They repeat in the technical process: one image is needed in two to four places at once. Thanks to the two-block structure, we printed about 400–500 of them in the book. I might be exaggerating the numbers, I don't remember well. But the book got much slimmer from that and became more interesting to look at. Essentially it's a database, like on the web. Form and content merged into a whole.

One more bit of know-how: no glue on the spine. It's fashionable now to make books without a spine, but they still use glue, and it feels sticky. It doesn't matter what Hermitage magazines I have with such a spine: you can still feel it. Or books from the Shrift publishing house. Many people play with this. And I didn't want glue, because it's visible, plus it's a limited edition, not a thousand copies. Together with the printing house we made a book with no glue at all. Paper, spine, everything velvety, thread holds it together. But it still needs to be glued, otherwise it falls apart: thread alone won't hold. So we fastened each signature to the next with double-sided tape, a two-millimeter strip. We searched for and bought a special durable tape. The craftsmen at the printing house remembered that project for a long time. But it turned out fire.

The book even won a prize at Best Book Design from all over the world in Germany. Later it brought me interface orders from geologists, strangely enough, people liked that a complex system was packaged in an elegant minimalist book. They came with interfaces and said: "Ours is a nightmare to figure out. Make it just as simple and beautiful."

DF

And how did the idea come about? The client came with a very clear request: change the font. Did the idea of making such a book come right away, or did you move iteratively?

YZ

First came the idea of how to do it better. If you're going to spend time on a project, you want to do it well. If an idea has come, it's criminal to turn it down and do worse than it could be.

I made a concept. I laid out about ten pages, put together a couple of shots in Photoshop on my knee. Not a real photo shoot, of course, but giving an idea: there will be a book where one side opens, with a box for it. I laid out a dozen pages with tables and engineering illustrations. There weren't really any references. I found cases where a book opens and two different blocks are glued into it. It's not super know-how, but it fits the idea.

The client liked the idea. Obviously the budget is different: for one job they asked you to change the text in a presentation, and for the other, making a thing like this. But they understood it was worth it. The unit cost is monstrous. But it's a gift edition, a small print run – the client could afford such an experiment.

I made the book alone – in about three to four months. There was no team then. Now I'd probably do something differently technically, but that's nitpicking and pure perfectionism.

DF

Tell me about another landmark project.

YZ

Well, I like this one. It's not so much about the project here as about working with a client in general. I collaborated with them for a very long time – about five years, and I made almost everything for them. When I came to them, they only had a tiny little logo. Everything else they wanted to redo. And we gradually redid all the design, all the branding, packaging, launched new brands, came up with branding for industry events. Email newsletter templates. Later I built an in-house web team for them, we hired developers, built a full digital design system, made a site with an online store and a product startup. In parallel I kept polishing communication design.

Their brands are super different in style – one coffee line, another more corporate, some industry branding. The client at first had no strategy – there was a vision, but how to get there, what to do, was unclear. And there were no gigantic budgets either. This is an example of how I led branding, both digital and communication, to a comprehensive solution through dozens of small iterations.

The first work was a little magazine, Barista Journal. Inside it – coffee cupping. Anyone who loves specialty coffee knows: it has all these flavor characteristics. Essentially we turned professional cupping into a friendly version for people who don't know the subject well. You don't have to be a Q-grader to evaluate a cup of the drink. You buy the little magazine, there's a system in it, a guide at the beginning on how to fill it in properly. You try a cup, you evaluate it on different parameters, and you have a diary like that.

When I came in, it seemed to me: God, what terrible nonsense, who in their right mind would rate coffee in a magazine, and pay for it? I designed it – it was the first project we made out of nothing, no branding, nothing. In the end it sold in crazy print runs. A super bestseller. I was pretty surprised then. And I realized that the world is much... I mean, you understand it with your head, you go: "Well, yeah, of course." But feeling it is a different experience. You see how much you only see a sliver of the world and of people, how little you understand them.

On this project I managed to widen my horizons of perception. To understand how different people are, how they can be in another culture, in another context, how important it is to treat that attentively. A tiny, tiny project, but it opened my eyes to the fact that design is, essentially, observation. First it's important not to spoil things. Like a doctor who runs diagnostics before operating. That's the foundation of product design in general, which later became so in the world and in my approach. But then it was a sudden discovery: good grief, you have to go not from your own prejudices but look at other people, observe how with minimal investment you can make their lives better, more convenient, more interesting, more beautiful.

A huge plus to the client – Chris, who acts as the agent for this company. He's a visionary, he believes in these things, finds them and says: "Okay, we're going there. And your job is to believe and go." And success happens.

Next – based on the success of the magazine, we understood what to do with fonts, that we wanted black and white and fonts of about that type, illustrations. We pulled in a flyer, a second font for it, then more illustrations, then an icon – something like a beige icon. Then the branding started to develop thread by thread, we ran a photo shoot, started thinking about a website. In parallel, industry events were launching: a business card for one, a poster for another, certificates for the barista school.

This is an interesting case where the business has no branding, and we don't approach it as a comprehensive, huge, immediately integral process like "let's take a year's budget and do it." That's how the principle I still use developed. The design of a project that has to live over time, a product or brand system, is built like this. There's a hard core, a center, DNA – like a gene, a genotype. It doesn't change. We know we have two or three principles: maybe about typography, maybe about the grid, maybe about something else. There should be enough of them for the visual core of communication to form.

And everything else is the phenotype of the project. It surrounds the gene and creates a soft shell that protects the core. This shell can be squeezed, tapped, and given any shape. Want it square, cubic, spherical, crumpled – whatever. That makes it very easy to build brand systems in dynamics and over time.

Which font do we use in the project? We don't care. Any. Everyone goes: "How can any font be okay in branding? Crime." And I say, it doesn't matter. We just haven't chosen yet. We don't know. Because in this presentation there's one, on the flyer a third, on the business card a fourth. Which to choose? Right now it's unclear. We need to research. Or it has to be bought for crazy money, and we're not ready yet. So let's make our decision, for now, to use any font. The main thing is to allow ourselves to be flexible.

Then the project can be developed gradually. Decisions gradually accumulate in the core. A year later – ah, that's it, we've settled on the font. Now we know what the posters are. Now we'll think about motion. The metaphor of a coffee bean: first it's green, then it's roasted, ground, brewed. And that turned into motion, then multiplied into a pattern, then was used on the site. A simple thing, but it didn't appear right away. It's useful for business – it helps build a solid foundation out of nothing, without big immediate investment. And flexibility remains.

DF

This differs from the usual approach, where a guide is written first and then everything is made to fit it. The idea that wholeness arrives over time seems new.

YZ

Here's the irony of any design work. You're right – cut it down and make it. But after everything is done, you can cut again, dig up something more and redo it. And in that sense the project turns out unfinished. It doesn't suit every client either – some are like: "What do you mean you want more money? We already made the site, the product, the branding." And you try to explain: we made a part. It's unlikely we'll do everything, if only because time will change, in a year your business will change, new challenges will appear. Development is needed.

This runs up against budgets and business goals. And it rarely works out to work this way, because it's not a project story. If you approach it project by project – let's make a notebook first, then we leave for half a year, come back, now flyers, now a website page – it's hard to achieve wholeness. Especially if it's not one designer working but often different ones. It's a long-term investment. Convenient in some ways, but there are downsides too. Essentially it's client support, and not all designers like that. Some like to do a project, close it, hand it over, get paid, and be free. That's also a great process in its own way, but it's hard to make things like this with it.

Any design that has to live over time has a hard core – a genotype that doesn't change. And a phenotype – a soft shell that can be squeezed into any shape.

– Yan Zaretsky
YZ

I'm actually not a very creative guy, not a creative designer, as I see it. I need a rational basis in any project. Until I find it, I'm stuck and projects go hard. I can't work in a creative space. I need to land. I need constraints.

In the service for geologists, for engineers and analysts who analyze deposits and design their development, first of all I drew the constraints. What I like in design: for example, in industrial design – think of Dieter Rams, Apple products, some devices. When it's a physical object, it has very clear constraints. It's clear how it's constructed, where the battery fits, where the processor goes, which buttons, how many, on the side or on top. If it's a competently made and thought-out system, it looks very beautiful. I like this technical aesthetic. And I love carrying that metaphor over to web interfaces.

Take the screen of the service for geologists. The left part is given to working with data. Movement from left to right: data → processing → visualization → customization. And all of it is subordinate to a set of tools. The compositional scheme of the entire interface expresses how the user works in the system. They don't have to jump around the interface and think about where this is, where that is. They know: all data sources are always in one place, all visualization in another.

This gives discipline in design. It is the core, the genotype of the project. We understand where a new feature should go. "Let's build a new calculator" – we know where it appears. "Let's add another chart" – no, the chart doesn't go here, that's visualization, we'll put it over there. Sometimes it's inconvenient, but it's the discipline the project needs, especially at stage zero. Later, when it starts adding features, it lets it survive, keep its wholeness. Otherwise by the tenth feature the interface turns into ten different places – it becomes unusable.

Everything starts with flowcharts. More than one of my projects is built this way. Sometimes it's for myself, sometimes I discuss it with the client – whether such rigidity is needed, whether it's rigid enough structurally, or needs to be more flexible.

Before we released this thing – it's in release now – we closed the user's basic need. A gigantic layer of work. There are definitely about a dozen steps from different tools. Import data, process it, calculate, recalculate, overlay, check, do something else with it. All the tools are laid out along the process: data, processing, analysis, interpretation, and decision. And only when we finished the last tools in the "Decisions" tab did the product gain practical use. Before that you could play around: do 50% of the work, even 90%, but not bring it to the end. This work took several years. Now it's accumulating new features.

Icons are a separate story. There are 900 of these little icons in the tree – nodes in the data. Fewer icons, because they repeat, but still about five hundred. Plus some merch, we styled a Telegram channel, posters.

Right now I work with neural networks quite actively – they let you make interactive prototypes quickly. Cursor, the same one. I know a little code, and it lets me, bypassing development, make little prototypes for different interactive behaviors. We have a lot of them in this thing: special clicks, special tools that work in a special way. It's not the web everyone knows. On the web you can sometimes even skip research – it's a generally accepted pattern, and it's clear anyway. Here there's no such thing. Here you're impressed at how unusual user interaction can be. Designers howl – they think it's trash, impossible to use. And geologists shout: it's the most convenient thing they've ever seen. Or the other way around. An interesting meeting of two worlds. And it's the same story as with the barista notebook, it's important to be an observer and carefully track the user's needs.

I'm not a very creative guy. I need a rational basis in any project. I need constraints.

– Yan Zaretsky
DF

Tell me about the practices and rituals around your work.

YZ

My main trait is that I really love personal contact and working in person. At the same time, I don't like offices and strict attendance. Even when I worked as a staff designer, the obligation to go to work was always a burden to me. Though with people it's great, you chat, it's interesting. When I went freelance and remote, God, what a happy time that was.

At the same time, I don't like fully remote work at all. All those designer dreams: I'll move to Bali and do my banking remotely on calls. That's not for me. It's fun for a month, maybe two. But I like meeting people, immersing, talking, including with clients. If we're talking about freelance or work – coming to the office, chatting, discussing ideas together – is a very valuable thing.

I came to this – or rather realized it – around 2015 or 2016. I was already like that, but didn't realize it right away. When I'd been fully freelance for several years, I understood: the lack of communication limits your experience very much. Critical assessment becomes different, your eye gets dulled. If there are no designers around to discuss with, and clients don't always understand it, then you really don't know how to evaluate your work. The client seems to accept it, and you yourself don't seem to like it – or like it. And it all starts to drift. Not a great state. Not to mention that you wake up, get up, and you're the same at home as at work. At first it's convenient, then not so much.

I always liked meeting. With all my clients I try to meet in person, discuss, talk about life, immerse in the business, and visit all the production sites when possible. They didn't let me into the engineers' plant, unfortunately, restricted facilities. I hope to ask again. And for the geological service I flew to Noyabrsk, talked, met the users, saw what they praise and what not so much. That's great.

That's how the Masterskaya project appeared. There are other designers with such needs – for whom remote work is hard. You want to cross-pollinate with ideas, share thoughts, concepts. Peek at each other's monitors. Sometimes ask questions. Recently I asked a colleague how his files are organized in Figma, a practical and rather boring thing, but even that is interesting to discuss. Not to mention approaches: is this cool or not, what's happening in the world, what about AI, messengers, what technologies are emerging. Not to mention the basic need of any designer – to tell someone: "Hey, look, is this crap or okay?" Sometimes you want to know. And there isn't always someone to ask. A senior person, a manager, an art director – good if you have one. But not everyone does, especially staff designers.

We came together in 2020 in the Masterskaya project, which I made then with Dima Bertolucci and Sergey Gurov. We rent a space, not tied by projects or jobs. Sometimes joint projects happen, but in general everyone has their own design life. Someone's a graphic designer, someone does apps for startups, someone is in a corporation. Absolutely different specializations, and that's great, because there's a different circle of acquaintances, a different view of the profession and experience.

Now there are five of us. Our own place, we hauled in computers, furniture, did everything as we wanted. Our own attendance, our own lunches, we work however we want. Everyone has their own requirements for their own projects. It's already the fifth place, I think. We invite guests, chat. It's not a forge of ideas, not a big project. But it super helps with development and has a formative influence. Sometimes we invite a workshop – there was a lettering workshop, non-designers came, just a creative evening among friends.

I like that the project is slowly growing. A little community of friends of the Masterskaya: someone works here, someone messes around sometimes, we go to events. In my view this is possible precisely because we're not a work team, not tied by business, not dependent on each other for how someone did a task or what salary they get. That gives the necessary freedom.

As a result of the meetings, about a year ago we came up with the idea of releasing a zine. There are big commercial projects, clients, the service for geologists. And there's – let's meet, draw letters, bang out a zine on a risograph, print it in one color. Playful stuff. So far two issues. One about letters, the second a visual study of rhythm: what rhythm is, what it can be. Everyone has their own: for some technical, for some artistic, for some positive – different approaches. A simple project, I like it. It's interesting even to product designers – to make offline design with your hands, which then gets printed and can be touched.

DF

What is quality, good design for you?

YZ

It's like both art and engineering at once. It should be whole, with nothing superfluous. There should be a basis – a concept, an idea, and everything is subordinate to it. If something drops out in a strange, inexplicable way, the design loses.

The more accidents in a project, the worse the design, as a rule. Accidents can be ones the designer didn't notice, didn't see, didn't know. From errors in composition to conceptual ones: picked an unfortunate color combination, a palette, something else. The more whole the project, the better. It can be complex inside, can be simple, doesn't matter. It can be a zine: one cover, one paper, one ink, and that's it. And there's super complex, composite, comprehensive branding that has to be supported across different media and still remain whole.

These are the criteria I apply to design – wholeness. I don't evaluate in terms of taste. It has to be something: either even on the grid, or playful, authorial, expressive, or necessarily solving a business task, or convenient. Who said design has to be convenient? I don't think so. Sometimes design should on the contrary be inconvenient. All of it should be subordinate to a larger goal.

And the larger goal is formed by the business and the user. And the designer – in my view, three points of attraction. Though this sounds a bit selfish, because it seems there are only the business and the user. And whose interests is the designer for? The business pays the money, there's nobody much to defend the user's interests – well, the wallet, perhaps, in a commercial story. But since design affects culture and people, the designer also bears responsibility for that. And it's acceptable for the designer to take on a bit of responsibility and the right to make decisions, because you can't dictate conditions if you don't take responsibility for the consequences.

Then there are three points of attraction – the business, the user, and the designer. And in this triangle each project finds its own coordinate. It sets the criteria of evaluation. Whether it worked out or not. Good or not. In which style. In which technology. Effective, convenient, economically justified – all of that depends. It's usually clarified in dialogue. With the business, with the client. The business doesn't always understand right away either what design it needs, that too is born in dialogue. Owners, product owners formulate: we're going there. Oh no, let's refine it. Let's go the other way. The designer deftly adjusts, models solutions, observes the business, the user, the culture around.

Perceiving it from the outside is another question. I openly don't criticize any colleagues – it's not very proper or prudent. To properly criticize design, it helps to understand the context in which it lives. Some criticism, in my view, isn't very professional. I try to collect the design I like around me. And the design I don't like, simply not to talk about it.

The more accidents in a project, the worse the design, as a rule.

– Yan Zaretsky
DF

Do you see a conflict between craft and technology, or do they reinforce each other?

YZ

Here I probably contradict myself somewhat. There's Jaron Lanier, who thinks that for the good of humanity we shouldn't use social networks at all – we should delete them all and not use them in the model of the internet we have now. I rather agree with him, though I use every social network I can.

And the same with AI, with AGI, with other technologies. How much good is in them and how it affects us. It seems to solve a task – at the same time it kills the ability to think and reason critically. That's all true. But I'm still for adopting new technologies, applying them, exploring them. Moving forward, just doing it wisely and carefully. I'm for progress.

It doesn't harm craft in any way, it changes it. If you look historically, it's not a new story at all. Book printing appeared. Okay, we no longer look at scrolls, no longer go to the town square to hear the people. We read a newspaper, then the internet. That changes the perception of the world. And it's dangerous in that society's perception is distorted through these tools – they're used both for good and not so much. That's normal, consistent human development. Let's just try to use them for good. As far as we can influence that – it seems that's the only power we have. To refuse is to lose. Resisting is useless. But to lead and do it well – that seems possible.

In that respect I'm not an optimist, no. It's a rather dystopian reality, because the machine of consumption is only spinning up. All our wonderful social networks – great, we chat with classmates, we follow our mom on Instagram. But at the same time all these engagement and marketing mechanics affect our brains, our values. The stance "I buy, therefore I am" is categorically alien to me and I don't like it. At the same time I understand that a good half of designers work for exactly that. We as designers happily report on product metrics. Oh, our TNPS went up. Oh, how great we are. Oh, we have engagement, we have retention – all that. Essentially this means the world is gradually sinking into a heap of illusory, contrived values that aren't real.

This is all about Baudrillard, Groys, Lanier, nobody talks about it, because it doesn't lead to the common human good, it rather moves away from it. That's how it is. But ignoring it is strange too. If there's a tool, then it should be used, just competently and well, and we should try to compensate for it with education.

To refuse technology is to lose. Resisting is useless. But to lead and do it well – that seems possible.

– Yan Zaretsky
