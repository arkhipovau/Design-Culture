Sergey Kudinov

Culture vs. Simulation in Product Design, and the Value of Manual Work

Russia • Moscow • Product

DF

What is design culture, and what does it mean to you?

SK

I'd separate two things: there's design in life in general, the design of life itself. And there's design culture at work. Let's start with work.

Depending on the people, the size of the organization and the tasks they solve, this culture may emerge, or it may get in the way. If by design culture you mean some bureaucratic, procedural stuff, a built process that everyone must follow – that's not culture. That's a simulation. Many people confuse design culture with a procedural thing that the whole team has to go through to get a result. I don't think so. I classify that as process.

Culture in a large organization is when people understand the importance of their own role and the importance of the neighboring role. There's trust, people trust expertise and think in terms of the result, not their own specific piece of work.

A common practice: a designer got a task from marketing, very specific. The designer did it, handed it over. And then it turns out it's a much broader thing. And it turns out it didn't need to be done at all. But when a person thinks: marketing gave it to me, I completed the task – nobody can hold me accountable. That's the absence of design culture.

Its presence is when a person asks questions, knows the overall goals, where we're heading, and evaluates their own role and contribution to the process. How effectively that role will perform and whether the contribution will be useful.

SK

At Yandex I see that design culture is very well developed. And not because it was an order from above, but because it grew out of the people. People understand what they're doing, and why. There's someone who thinks about the "why." That's important.

When I came to Yandex, I saw that everyone is curious: why are you doing this? Not just do it. It's healthy skepticism. This culture lets you voice your opinion even if you're a designer and you were asked something else.

Design culture is, first of all, honesty with yourself. Honesty about what you do. Why are you doing this? If you have a task, you should rethink it. Not just carry it out, but understand whether it's the right one. That takes courage. Because the client may not want to hear that the task isn't quite right.

It's also respect for the people you work with. Understanding that every person has their own expertise, and you should listen to it.

And third is the result. Not just process, but specifically the result. Design culture is built around all of us together making something people need. Design isn't just pretty pictures. Design is problem solving. If you apply this principle to life, you become better.

At work you come in in the morning and need to solve some problem. If you approach it from a designer's position, you'll think not only about yourself but about how it will affect the team, the company, the user. That's design culture.

In a large organization there are always systems and processes. And they matter. But they shouldn't be above culture. I've seen companies where processes crushed people. They just follow some path and don't think about why they're doing it.

And at Yandex processes serve culture, not the other way around. People use processes to be more effective, not to meet formal requirements.

If you have process but no culture – that's bureaucracy. If you have culture but no process – that's chaos. You need both.

Design culture depends on how people lead others. If you have a leader who just gives orders and doesn't listen, there won't be culture. But if you have a leader who listens, who explains why it needs to be done this way, and is ready to change their view if convinced otherwise – that creates culture.

The leaders I work with at Yandex aren't afraid to voice an opposing opinion. They're ready to listen. That strongly influenced how I understand design culture myself.

Design culture in a team is built on trust and honest communication. When I joined the team, I was new. And I saw that the guys were very open to discussion. They were ready to help. They were ready to explain why they do something one way and not another. That creates a feeling that you're not just doing your job, you're part of a team solving a common problem.

In a good design culture, mistakes aren't something terrible. Mistakes are part of the learning process. I know that if I make a mistake, I'll be helped to fix it. And I'll learn something new. That's different from cultures where a mistake is grounds for punishment. At Yandex mistakes are seen as a chance to improve the system. Not to scold a person, but to understand why it happened and what can be changed. That creates a safe space for experiments and innovation.

But the core of the culture remains. It's honesty, trust, effectiveness. When I see a company that managed to preserve these values despite growth and change – that I respect.

Design culture isn't something static. It's a living process. Companies change, people change, tasks change. And culture has to adapt to those changes.

– Sergey Kudinov
DF

Books, films, people who influenced you?

SK

I read a lot, watch a lot. But the biggest influence was life.

When I saw companies that built a good culture, I learned. When I saw companies that didn't, I learned from their mistakes.

There are classic books on design. There are people who write and talk a lot about culture. But the most important thing is to see it in action. At Yandex I see it in action: how people value each other, how they listen, how they build something together. That's the best textbook.

DF

How do you feel about neural networks and AI in design?

SK

An interesting question. I see neural networks appearing in all fields, including design.

At the current level there is some benefit. They save time. But I don't see artistic value. When I look at the results of neural networks, I immediately see neural-network generation.

I find it more pleasant to look at manual work. That's an artist drawing. And if an artist draws like a neural network, so soullessly, I find it incredibly unpleasant to look at.

In short, for now artificial intelligence is just one of the tools. People who know how to use these tools and look at things broadly will be valued. They won't replace us. Until real artificial intelligence appears. When it will, I don't know.
