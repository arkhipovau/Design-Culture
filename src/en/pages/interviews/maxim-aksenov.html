Maxim Aksenov

Architectural background, design as a system, and design as a discipline of thinking

Russia • Moscow • Freelance

DF

How do you define “design culture” for yourself? What does the term include: principles, values, rituals within a team, an approach to decision-making, responsibility, or something else? Basically, it comes down to ideology.

MA

To be honest, I have never asked myself this question, so it was really interesting to think about it.

For me, design culture is first of all about thinking, not visuals. It is about how you work with a task, how you discuss solutions, how you take criticism, and how willing you are to rebuild your work for a more precise result.

As for principles, the main one for me is design with meaning. It should be clear, informative, and convey the brand’s idea or concept accurately, rather than boiling down to a pretty picture assembled from current trends.

Another principle follows from this: the pursuit of timeless design. It matters to me to make solutions that do not depend on short-term trends and stay relevant thanks to a clear idea and a well-built visual system.

Honesty in design is also fundamental for me: when the visual language does not mask a weak concept but strengthens a strong one. And, in general, respect for the content and for the user matters: design should not complicate things or mislead.

In the end, design culture for me is not only about form but about a discipline of thinking and the ability to make the complex understandable without losing touch with the meaning originally embedded in it.

DF

What is behind your architectural background? Tell us about your path and how it influences your work now.

MA

My love for graphic design began in 2007, when I was in my first year at the Moscow Architectural Institute (MARCHI). In one of the architectural composition classes, I caught myself thinking that I was especially interested in working with type: creating typographic compositions, looking for visual rhymes, rethinking form, and achieving expressiveness through it.

From that moment I started diving deeper into graphic design: breaking down and analyzing case studies, copying techniques, trying to understand why certain solutions work for particular tasks. It was important to me not just to look but to understand the logic behind visual decisions.

By the end of my studies at MARCHI, I had developed not only an architectural eye but a design eye as well. And this symbiosis, probably, largely shaped my current style and approach to design. In my work I often rely on architectural principles: structure, rhythm, symmetry, working with space. In general, I see design as a system rather than a set of visual effects.

DF

Which practices and guiding points in your work stay unchanged? What helps and inspires you outside of projects?

MA

The research approach stays unchanged: first understand the context, the task, and the logic, and only then move on to form. It is important for me to work step by step, from the general to the specific, from structure to details.

Outside of projects, almost everything that happens to me and every process around me is inspiring. But if I had to pick one thing, it would definitely be travel. The visual experience I get on trips is hard to compare with anything. I like immersing myself in different cities, absorbing their architecture and structure, studying typography, signage design, utilitarian design, and, in general, how the visual environment is put together. For me, it is one of the strongest sources of inspiration.

DF

About LASH: what is the most important thing for you in this project, and why do you keep coming back to it?

MA

For me, the main thing in the LASH project is working with a team that has a very strong foundation of meaning and concept. This is exactly the case where it is important to make visual decisions that truly match the level of the ideas behind them.

Honestly, their thinking is not always immediately clear to me, but that is exactly where the special interest lies: figuring it out, digging in, and searching for the precise visual image for these meanings. Every time it becomes a kind of professional challenge: translating complex content into a clear, precise, and accessible visual language.

This is where I see the main value of the project for myself, and this is exactly why I want to return to it again and again.

DF

Are there projects where your values and design decisions aligned especially closely? Tell us about your favorite recent case.

MA

I like how the identity of the summer architecture school turned out. The project is quite spontaneous and required quick decisions, a sort of express design, but that does not make it any less valuable; on the contrary, it becomes even more valuable to me.

A little backstory on how the school’s identity has transformed over time. Every year LASH has a new research theme. In 2024 it was “Movement in Architecture.” Working on the visual part that year, we tried out different solutions, typefaces, and layout principles, and in the end we developed a graphic mark, the LASH monogram, and arrived at a monospaced typeface that, in our view, connects well with the historical context and with architecture in general, thanks to its slenderness and the clear positioning of the glyphs relative to one another.

MA

In 2025 the theme was “Color in Architecture,” and the result was the core elements of the brand style: the logo, the typeface, and the layout principles in the designs. These will be kept and remain unchanged going forward. The visual imagery, techniques, palette, and additional style-defining compositional elements, by our decision, will transform depending on the school’s current research theme.

MA

This year the theme is “Time in Architecture,” and my task was to find a visual image that would accurately convey the sense of time in architecture. The result is a visual concept made up of two stylistic solutions: first, typography that fragmentarily disappears, as an image of time slipping away from us; second, a blurred geometric shape as an image of an object or building dissolving against the typography, a reference to memories that blur and lose sharpness over time. In the end, the combination of these two techniques creates a sense of spatial depth and, at the same time, of a new and an old layer of time.

DF

Craft and technology (AI): how is your relationship with new tools shaping up, and have they become part of your routine?

MA

Craft remains the foundation of my work. It is still important for me to go through the whole path myself: from the idea and structure to typography, composition, and the final result. It is in this process that precision and a sense of authorship appear.

I approach new tools calmly and with interest. For me, AI is not a replacement for design thinking but an additional layer that helps speed up exploration, try different directions, run tests, and get to workable options faster.

Over time these tools became part of my routine quite naturally. I use them where they really help: in research, in generating intermediate ideas, in visual experiments, or in preparing variations.

But the key decisions still stay with me. It is important to me that technology does not replace taste, a trained eye, and a sense of proportion. So AI is a useful tool, but not a value in itself. What is most interesting to me still happens at the moment when technology meets manual selection, attention to detail, and design logic.
